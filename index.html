Formula sheet

Everything you need to compute by hand on the midterm, in one place.

One random variable

\(E(X)=\sum_i x_i\,\Pr(X=x_i)\)

\(\mathrm{var}(X)=E[(X-\mu_X)^2]=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\)

\(E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\)   \(\mathrm{var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{var}(X)\)

Dummy: \(E(D)=p\), \(\mathrm{var}(D)=p(1-p)\)

Two random variables

\(\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=E(XY)-\mu_X\mu_Y\)

\(\mathrm{corr}(X,Y)=\dfrac{\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\in[-1,1]\)

\(\Pr(Y=y\mid X=x)=\Pr(X=x,Y=y)/\Pr(X=x)\)

\(\mathrm{cov}(aX+b,cY+d)=ac\,\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)\)

\(\mathrm{var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{var}X+\mathrm{var}Y+2\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)\)

Sample mean

\(E(\bar Y)=\mu_Y\), \(\mathrm{var}(\bar Y)=\sigma_Y^2/n\)

CLT: \(\bar Y\approx N(\mu_Y,\sigma_Y^2/n)\)

\(s_Y^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2\), \(SE(\bar Y)=s_Y/\sqrt n\)

Proportion: \(SE(\hat p)=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\)

Tests and intervals

\(t=\dfrac{\text{estimate}-\text{hypothesized value}}{SE}\)

Two-sided \(p=2\Phi(-|t|)\); one-sided (\(>\)) \(p=\Phi(-t)\)

CI = estimate \(\pm\, c\times SE\); \(c=1.645, 1.96, 2.576\)

Reject at 5% \(\iff |t|>1.96 \iff p<0.05 \iff\) value outside 95% CI

Two groups

\(SE(\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0)=\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_0^2/n_0}\)

Observed difference = effect on the treated + selection bias

\(E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]+\{E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\}\)

Take-up and spillovers

First stage = share treated if offered − share treated if not

Effect of treatment = effect of offer ÷ first stage

Treat − control = effect on treated − change in control

OLS

\(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}=\dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}\)

\(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X\) (line goes through \((\bar X,\bar Y)\))

\(\sum\hat u_i=0\), \(\sum X_i\hat u_i=0\)

Dummy X: \(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y_0\), \(\hat\beta_1=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0\)

Fit

TSS = ESS + SSR

\(R^2=ESS/TSS=1-SSR/TSS\)

\(SER=\sqrt{SSR/(n-2)}\) (Stata "Root MSE")

Bivariate: \(R^2=\mathrm{corr}(X,Y)^2\)

Logs

Linear-log \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1\ln X\): 1% ↑X → \(0.01\beta_1\) units of Y

Log-linear \(\ln Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X\): 1 unit ↑X → \(100\beta_1\)% of Y

Log-log: 1% ↑X → \(\beta_1\)% of Y (elasticity)

Properties of \(\hat\beta_1\)

\(\hat\beta_1=\beta_1+\dfrac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)u_i}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\)

\(\hat\beta_1\xrightarrow{p}\beta_1+\dfrac{\mathrm{cov}(X_i,u_i)}{\mathrm{var}(X_i)}\)

Homoskedastic \(SE(\hat\beta_1)\approx SER/(\sqrt n\,s_X)\); always report robust SEs

The one story the whole course tells

The midterm tests concepts more than math. Every lecture so far answers one question: when does a comparison in data tell us what causes what? Here is the path, one step at a time.

L1 · The question

Data shows things moving together (association). Policy needs to know what happens if we change something (causation). These are different, and confusing them leads to bad policy, like handing out Mozart CDs.

L2–L3 · The language of uncertainty

We describe the world with random variables. The expected value is the long-run average, the variance is the spread, and covariance says whether two things move together. Our estimators are random too: a different sample gives a different number.

L4 · Separating signal from noise

Because estimates bounce around from sample to sample, we ask: is this result bigger than chance would produce? The standard error measures the bounce, and the t-statistic and p-value measure how surprising our result would be if nothing were going on. This deals with noise, not with bias.

L5 · What "causal" means

A causal effect compares the same person in two worlds, only one of which we ever see. Comparing two different groups gives the effect plus selection bias: the groups were different to begin with. A lottery removes selection bias.

L6–L7 · Regression is a comparison of averages

Comparing two group means is a regression on a dummy. With a variable taking many values, least squares draws the line that best summarizes how the average of Y changes with X. Everything else that affects Y sits in the error term u.

L8 · How well does the line fit?

R² and the SER describe fit. Fit is a different question from causation: a regression can fit badly and still give a clean causal effect (Oregon), or fit well and be badly biased.

L9 · When can we trust the slope?

The slope is right on average and gets closer with more data only if the error term has nothing systematic to do with X: \(E[u\mid X]=0\). More data shrinks noise but never fixes bias.

L10 · What goes wrong: omitted variables

If something left in u affects Y and moves with X, the slope picks up its effect. That is omitted variable bias, and you can predict its direction from two signs.

L11 · The fix we can try: multiple regression

Put the omitted variable into the regression and compare observations that share its value. This removes bias from the variables we include, never from the ones we leave out.

Almost any concept question can be answered by asking three things in order: (1) What is being compared? (2) What else differs between the groups being compared (what is in u)? (3) Does that make the estimate too big or too small?

Lecture 1 · Wed Sep 9

Introduction: what econometrics is for

Reading: S&W ch. 1; Mastering 'Metrics, Introduction.

Key ideas

  • Econometrics is a set of statistical techniques for examining empirical (data-based) relationships between variables. We use a sample to make inferences about relationships in the population.
  • Course goal: critically assess the quality and credibility of empirical studies, and critically read the news.
  • Two kinds of relationships. Causal: the effect that changing X has on Y. Association: how much two variables move together, which is enough for prediction and description. The question asked all semester: is an association we see actually causal?
  • Causal vs. prediction questions. "Will a rain dance lower the chance of rain?" is causal; "Will it rain tomorrow?" is prediction. Historically econometrics focused on causality, machine learning on prediction; the two are converging.

Types of data

  • Unstructured: text, images, audio, web pages. Not a table; must be turned into one (count words, classify images) before econometrics can use it.
  • Cross-section: many units observed once (420 California school districts in 1999).
  • Panel / longitudinal: the same units observed at several times (48 US states, 1982–1988, beer tax and traffic deaths).

Experiments vs. observational data

  • Randomized experiments are the gold standard: flip a coin for each patient, half get the drug and half a placebo, compare. Statistics tells you whether the difference is bigger than chance.
  • Experiments are not always feasible (cost, administration, ethics) and may not answer the policy question. So we often use observational data (surveys, administrative records), where the variable of interest is not randomly assigned and we need econometric techniques to get causal estimates.
  • Course arc: (1) randomized experiments, (2) multiple regression, (3) quasi-experiments: instrumental variables, panel data, differences-in-differences, regression discontinuity.

Examples from class

  • El Salvador's AI tutors (Grok-based tutor in 171 pilot schools, part of a $500M World Bank reform also adding lesson plans, coaching, remediation and equipment). The pilot had 1,198 volunteer students: can you learn the effect for the whole country from volunteers, and can you separate the tutor from the rest of the package?
  • Mozart effect (Rauscher, Shaw & Ky 1993): 36 college students, 10 minutes of Mozart, spatial-reasoning subtests rose 8–9 IQ points, gone within 10–15 minutes. Georgia then gave classical CDs to all newborns' parents. Verdict: probably a bad policy. Different population (newborns vs. college students), different outcome (general intelligence vs. one spatial task), different time horizon (lasting vs. 15 minutes); later replications largely failed. This is an external validity problem.
  • Fossil fuels and life expectancy: positively associated across countries, but that does not mean fossil fuels raise life expectancy (both rise with income).
  • Gender and race pay gaps: raw gaps are meaningful associations, but "the causal effect of being a Hispanic woman" mixes pay discrimination with pre-market differences (education, field). The policy answer depends on which part you mean.

When asked whether a study supports a policy, check three things: is the estimate causal for the people studied (internal validity), and does it carry to the policy's population, outcome and time horizon (external validity)?

Concept checks

A study finds that students who use an AI tutor score higher. Name one reason this may not be causal and one reason it may not carry to all schools.

Not causal: volunteers who opt in may be more motivated (selection bias). May not carry over: 171 pilot schools with extra coaching and equipment are a different setting from 5,000 schools at scale (external validity).

Is 'Will this student pass?' a causal or a prediction question? What about 'Would tutoring make this student pass?'

The first is prediction; the second is causal (it asks about a counterfactual change).

Cross-section or panel: 50 states' unemployment rates each month from 2000 to 2020?

Panel: the same units observed at many points in time.

Lecture 2 · Mon Sep 14

Probability I: random variables, expected value, variance

Reading: S&W ch. 2.

Motivating study: AI and lawyers (Autor et al. 2026)

  • 133 lawyers at 11 IP law firms randomized within each firm and separately by experience, 2:1 treatment to control (90 vs. 43). Treated got InFlow, an AI drafting assistant, for 3 months.
  • Why randomize within firm? Firms differ; compare like with like. Why separately by experience? Keeps juniors and seniors balanced. Why 2:1? The firms wanted more lawyers to get AI; it costs precision.
  • Outcomes scored by blinded attorneys, standardized to control mean 0, SD 1 ("SD units"). Specification: \(Y_{ik}=\alpha+\beta_1T_i+\gamma_j+\delta_k+\varepsilon_{ik}\); \(\beta_1\) is the causal effect of AI access.
  • Results: drafting with AI +0.34 SD (day 10), +0.38 SD (day 90); juniors gained more (+0.58/+0.60) than seniors. Redlining without AI: +0.32 SD overall, but all among seniors (+0.45); juniors −0.03. "The largest gains from AI accrued to the lawyers who retained the least."

Random variables

  • A random variable is a quantity that depends on random events. Uppercase \(X\) for the variable, lowercase \(x\) for a realized value.
  • Discrete (a countable set of values, e.g. number of students at class) vs. continuous (a continuum, e.g. temperature). π or the speed of light are not random variables.
  • The probability distribution of a discrete RV lists every value and its probability: "everything that can happen and how likely each outcome is." One die: each of 1–6 has prob 1/6. Sum of two dice: 2,…,12 with probs 1/36, 2/36, …, 6/36 (at 7), …, 1/36.
  • A continuous RV has a probability density function; the area under the curve between two values is the probability of landing there. Normal: about 68% within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2.

Expected value

\[E(X)=\sum_{i=1}^k x_i\Pr(X=x_i)\qquad\text{(continuous: }E(Y)=\textstyle\int y f(y)\,dy)\]
  • A probability-weighted average. It is a constant, not a random variable, and need not be a possible outcome (die: \(E(X)=21/6=3.5\)).
  • It is the long-run average: rolling a die 1,000 times, the running average went 6, 3.5, 2.67, … 3.10 after 20, 3.41 after 100, 3.51 after 1,000. Different runs take different paths to the same limit.
  • Also called the expectation or population mean, written \(\mu_Y\).
  • St. Petersburg paradox: flip until heads; payout \(\$2^k\) if the first head is on flip \(k\), with prob \(1/2^k\). Each row contributes \(2^k\cdot 2^{-k}=1\), so \(E=1+1+1+\cdots=\infty\), yet nobody pays much to play.

Variance and standard deviation

\[\mathrm{var}(X)=\sum_i(x_i-\mu_X)^2\Pr(X=x_i)=E[(X-\mu_X)^2]\qquad \sigma_X=\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(X)}\]
  • Recipe: distance from the mean, squared, weighted by probability, summed. Larger variance means more spread and more uncertainty.
  • Why square? So positive and negative deviations don't cancel (and large misses count more). Variance can never be negative. Its units are squared (dollars²), which is why we take the square root: the SD is in the original units.

Properties (proved in PS1)

ExpectationsVariances
\(E(c)=c\)\(\mathrm{var}(c)=0\)
\(E(X+Y)=E(X)+E(Y)\) (always)\(\mathrm{var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{var}X+\mathrm{var}Y\) (if independent)
\(E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\)\(\mathrm{var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{var}(X)\)

Adding a constant shifts the mean but does not change the variance. Multiplying by \(a\) multiplies the variance by \(a^2\), not \(a\) (and the SD by \(|a|\)).

From your notes

  • Randomness = an outcome that cannot be predicted; probability quantifies it. Not random variables: π, the speed of light, any constant.
  • Expected value is the long-run average.
  • Larger variance ⇒ more spread ⇒ more uncertainty. The SD is always in the same units as the mean.
  • var(X + Y) = var X + var Y only if independent (you flagged this in red; keep it).

Concept checks

X takes 0 with prob .5 and 10 with prob .5. Find E(X), var(X), and var(3X + 4).

E(X) = 5. var(X) = .5(25) + .5(25) = 25. var(3X + 4) = 9 × 25 = 225 (the +4 does nothing).

Can E(X) be a value X never takes? Example?

Yes. A die has E(X) = 3.5.

If X is in dollars, what are the units of var(X) and of σX?

Dollars squared; dollars.

Why is the St. Petersburg bet's expected value infinite?

Each outcome k contributes 2k × (1/2)k = 1, and there are infinitely many outcomes, so the sum 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯ never stops growing.

Lecture 3 · Wed Sep 16

Probability II: two variables, samples and estimators

Reading: S&W ch. 2–3.

Warm-up: reading Table 2 of the lawyers paper

  • Day-10 drafting: treated 0.37, control 0.00, so the implied effect is 0.37 SD.
  • Why is the control mean exactly 0? Each score is standardized with the control mean and SD, and the pooled score averages five such scores. Why is the control SD about 0.9, not 1? Averaging shrinks spread; five perfectly correlated scores would give 1, so 0.9 says they are highly but not perfectly correlated.
  • 90-day differences: drafting juniors \(0.36-(-0.32)=0.68\), seniors \(0.50-0.13=0.37\); redlining juniors \(-0.06-0.02=-0.08\), seniors \(0.38-(-0.01)=0.39\). Juniors gain most while AI is on and keep nothing once it is off.
  • Junior redlining: control SD 0.52 vs. treated 1.02 with nearly the same mean. Same center, twice the spread: AI "bifurcated" juniors. But only 14 control ratings, so that SD is noisy (paper's test of spread: p = 0.06).

Joint, marginal, conditional

  • Joint probability \(\Pr(X=x,Y=y)\): the cells. Marginal: row or column totals. Example: \(\Pr(\text{college grad, employed})=0.389\); \(\Pr(\text{employed})=0.965\).
  • Conditional: \(\Pr(Y=y\mid X=x)=\Pr(X=x,Y=y)/\Pr(X=x)\). Independent if \(\Pr(X=x,Y=y)=\Pr(X=x)\Pr(Y=y)\) for every cell.
  • Conditional expectation \(E[Y\mid X=x]=\sum_j y_j\Pr(Y=y_j\mid X=x)\): the mean of Y among units with \(X=x\) (expected earnings among people with 16 years of school).

Covariance and correlation

\[\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y,\qquad \mathrm{corr}(X,Y)=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\]
  • Positive covariance: when X is above its mean, Y tends to be above its mean. \(\mathrm{cov}(X,X)=\mathrm{var}(X)\). Units: units of X × units of Y.
  • Problem: covariance depends on units (education in months instead of years multiplies it by 12; earnings in cents by 100).
  • If \(Y=a+bX\), then \(\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)=b\,\mathrm{var}(X)\). Two ways to strip out the spread of X: divide by \(\sigma_X\sigma_Y\) to get the correlation (unit-free, how strong), or divide by \(\mathrm{var}(X)\) to get \(b\) (how much Y moves per unit of X).
  • Correlation is between −1 and 1 and measures linear association only: Y can depend on X with zero correlation (S&W Fig. 2.10d).
  • If \(E[Y\mid X=x]=a+bx\), then \(b=\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{var}(X)\). This \(b\) becomes the regression coefficient. The "how much" question is a difference in conditional expectations: \(E[Y\mid X=16]-E[Y\mid X=12]=4b\).

Shortcut for two 0/1 variables: \(E(XY)=\Pr(X=1,Y=1)\), so \(\mathrm{cov}=\Pr(X=1,Y=1)-\Pr(X=1)\Pr(Y=1)\), and \(\mathrm{var}=p(1-p)\).

From populations to samples

  • We don't know population distributions, so we collect data as clues about them. Two worlds: the population (what we want) and the sample (what we have).
  • The course treats the population as a process (a data-generating process, DGP), not a finite list: another class of graduates drawn from the same rule gives a different average.
  • Estimand: the population parameter we want (e.g. \(\mu\)), fixed and unknown. Estimator: a rule applied to the random sample, so itself a random variable (e.g. \(\bar Y\)). Estimate: the number the rule returns for our data, not random once observed (e.g. $78,400).
  • i.i.d.: independent (one draw tells nothing about another; roommate pairs violate this) and identically distributed (all from the same population; mixing Columbia and NYU, or Dean's List only, violates this).

Properties of estimators

  • Any rule is an estimator; the question is whether it is good. Five-GPA example (3.2, 3.8, 3.5, 2.9, 3.6): mean 3.40, first obs 3.20, median 3.50, midrange 3.35, \(\frac14\sum Y_i=4.25\), \(\bar Y+0.1=3.50\).
  • Unbiased: \(E(\hat\mu)=\mu\), right on average across repeated samples. \(\bar Y\) and \(Y_1\) are unbiased; \(\frac14\sum\) (expected \(\frac54\mu\)) and \(\bar Y+0.1\) are biased up; median and midrange are unbiased only for symmetric distributions. Rule: linear in the \(Y_i\) with weights summing to one and no constant ⇒ unbiased.
  • Precision: among unbiased estimators prefer the smaller variance. \(\mathrm{var}(Y_1)=\sigma^2\) but \(\mathrm{var}(\bar Y)=\sigma^2/n\).
  • Consistent: \(\hat\mu\xrightarrow{p}\mu\); for any \(\varepsilon>0\), \(\Pr(|\hat\mu-\mu|>\varepsilon)\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\). Polling 10 vs. 1,000 vs. 1,000,000 people.

Derivation: mean and variance of \(\bar Y\)

\[E(\bar Y)=\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum E(Y_i)=\tfrac1n\,n\mu_Y=\mu_Y\] \[\mathrm{var}(\bar Y)=\tfrac{1}{n^2}\textstyle\sum_i\sum_j\mathrm{cov}(Y_i,Y_j)=\tfrac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\sigma_Y^2=\dfrac{\sigma_Y^2}{n}\quad(\mathrm{cov}(Y_i,Y_j)=0\text{ for } i\neq j \text{ by independence})\]

Know this derivation: it is the cleanest example of where independence is used. Quadruple \(n\) to halve the SD of \(\bar Y\).

From your notes

  • Covariance conflates slope and spread: if Y = a + bX, cov(X, Y) = b var(X). (Good phrasing; it is exactly why we divide by var(X) to get the slope.)
  • Correlation is unitless, between −1 and 1, and measures linear association only.
  • Conditional expectation E[Y | X = x] is the predicted value of Y given X, and its slope is b = cov(X, Y)/var(X).
  • Estimand (unknown parameter) → sample (Y₁…Yₙ) → estimator (a rule, a random variable) → estimate (a number, not random). Good samples are i.i.d.

Concept checks

You rescale earnings from dollars to thousands of dollars. What happens to cov(educ, earnings) and to corr(educ, earnings)?

Covariance is divided by 1,000. Correlation is unchanged (unit-free).

Two variables have correlation 0. Are they independent?

Not necessarily. Correlation only rules out a linear relationship; Y = X² with X symmetric around 0 has zero correlation but Y depends on X. (Independence does imply zero correlation.)

Is Ỹ = (Y₁ + Y₂)/2, using only the first two observations, unbiased for μ? Is it consistent?

Unbiased: E(Ỹ) = μ. Not consistent: its variance is σ²/2 forever, no matter how large n is.

A sample of only husband-wife pairs: which part of i.i.d. fails?

Independence: picking one spouse determines the other.

Lecture 4 · Mon Sep 21

Large samples and statistical inference

Reading: S&W 2.5–2.6 and ch. 3.

Two theorems about averages

  • Most estimators are averages or functions of averages: sample mean, sample variance (average of squared deviations), sample covariance (average of products), and the regression slope (a ratio of two averages).
  • Law of large numbers (one sample, \(n\) growing): \(\bar Y\xrightarrow{p}E[Y]\). Also \(s_Y^2\xrightarrow{p}\sigma_Y^2\) and \(s_{XY}\xrightarrow{p}\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)\). So \(\bar Y\) is consistent.
  • Central limit theorem (many samples, \(n\) fixed): if \(Y_i\) are i.i.d. with \(0<\sigma_Y^2<\infty\), then \(\dfrac{\bar Y-\mu_Y}{\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(\bar Y)}}\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\), whatever the shape of the population. Equivalently \(\bar Y\approx N(\mu_Y,\sigma_Y^2/n)\). Rule of thumb: \(n>30\), "a rule of thumb, not a law."
  • Die at \(n=30\): \(\sigma=1.708\), \(SD(\bar Y)=1.708/\sqrt{30}=0.312\), so 95% of averages land in \(3.5\pm1.96(0.312)=[2.89,4.11]\). The bell is useless at \(n=1\), close at \(n=5\), on the bars at \(n=30\).

The LLN is about where \(\bar Y\) goes (a point). The CLT is about the shape of its distribution (a bell). Don't swap them.

Hypothesis testing in six steps

  1. State two claims. \(H_0:\mu_Y=\mu_0\) vs. \(H_1:\mu_Y\neq\mu_0\) (or one-sided \(>\), \(<\)). \(H_0\) is the default, presumed true like a defendant; the burden of proof is on the claim (Russell's teapot).
  2. Pretend \(H_0\) is true. By the CLT, \(z=(\bar Y-\mu_0)/(\sigma_Y/\sqrt n)\approx N(0,1)\): the null distribution.
  3. Locate your \(\bar Y\) in it: \(z^{obs}\) is its distance from \(\mu_0\) in SDs of \(\bar Y\).
  4. Decide at significance level \(\alpha\) fixed in advance: reject if \(|z^{obs}|>c_\alpha\) (1.645 / 1.96 / 2.576 for 10 / 5 / 1%, shown as * / ** / ***). Otherwise "fail to reject," never "accept."
  5. p-value: \(p=\Pr_{H_0}(|z|\ge|z^{obs}|)=2\Phi(-|z^{obs}|)\). Same verdict as step 4 (reject iff \(p<\alpha\)).
  6. The catch: \(\sigma_Y\) is unknown, so use \(s_Y\): \(SE(\bar Y)=s_Y/\sqrt n\), and \(z\) becomes the t-statistic. For large \(n\), still \(\approx N(0,1)\).
  • Type I error: rejecting a true \(H_0\); its probability is \(\alpha\). Type II error: failing to reject a false \(H_0\). Smaller \(\alpha\) means fewer Type I and more Type II errors; there is no right number.
  • Say it right: "If \(H_0\) were true, an average at least this far from \(\mu_0\) would occur with probability \(p\)." Not "the probability that \(H_0\) is true is \(p\)."

Confidence intervals

  • The 95% CI is the set of \(\mu_0\) values that would not be rejected at 5%: \(\bar Y\pm1.96\,SE(\bar Y)\).
  • Meaning: across repeated samples, 95% of intervals built this way contain \(\mu_Y\). Any one interval either contains it or not; the 95% describes the procedure.

Worked examples from class

  • Is the die fair? 30 rolls average 4.2. \(z=(4.2-3.5)/0.312=2.24\), \(p=2\Phi(-2.24)=0.025\). Reject at 5% (2.24 > 1.96) but not at 1% (2.24 < 2.576). 95% CI \(4.2\pm0.61=[3.59,4.81]\) excludes 3.5.

Causality, potential outcomes and experiments

Reading: Mastering 'Metrics ch. 1; S&W 13.1–13.2.

The counterfactual

  • A causal effect is the change in Y due to X alone. Causal effect = observed outcome − counterfactual outcome, where the counterfactual is the world that did not happen. We never observe both for the same unit at the same time.
  • Before/after comparisons fail (emissions rules): other things changed, and those things may be why the policy was adopted.
  • Goal of causal econometrics: mimic the counterfactual. Best case: build a comparable control group by randomizing. Usual case: find one in observational data.

Potential outcomes

  • \(D_i\) = treatment (1 if insured). \(Y_{1i}\) = outcome if treated, \(Y_{0i}\) = outcome if not. Observed: \(Y_i=Y_{0i}+(Y_{1i}-Y_{0i})D_i\). Individual effect \(Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}\), never observed.
  • Khuzdar (insured, Y = 4) vs. Maria (uninsured, Y = 5): naive gap −1. Filling in the unseen: \(Y_{0K}=3\), so insurance raised Khuzdar's health by +1, but he started 2 points behind Maria (\(3-5=-2\)). \(-1=+1+(-2)\).
  • Target: the average causal effect \(E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}]\).

The key decomposition

\[\underbrace{E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{observed difference}}=\underbrace{E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]}_{\text{effect on the treated}}+\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]-E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}\]

Derived by adding and subtracting \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]\), the health the insured would have had without insurance, which no survey measures. The data give only the total.

  • Selection bias is how treated and untreated would compare if nobody were treated: the head start, not an effect of treatment.
  • NHIS: education and employment raise \(E[Y_{0i}\mid\text{insured}]\) → positive bias, 0.31 overstates. Age pushes the other way. The net sign is a judgment.
  • "Coffee drinkers live longer": the already-sick are told to stop → positive bias, overstated (smokers drink more coffee, so the sign is an argument). "Dog owners are happier": owners have houses, yards, settled lives → positive bias.

Why randomization works

A lottery makes \(D_i\) independent of potential outcomes: \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i]=E[Y_{0i}]\) and \(E[Y_{1i}\mid D_i]=E[Y_{1i}]\). Two consequences: (1) selection bias = 0; (2) the treated are a random draw, so the effect on the treated equals the average causal effect. With large groups this holds by the LLN.

\[\text{observed difference}=E[Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}]+0\]

  • The treatment is winning, not enrolling. Comparing enrollees with non-enrollees puts choice back in. Winners vs. losers is the only split a coin made.

  • Internal: is the estimate right for the people studied? Oregon: yes, by randomization.
  • External: does it carry to other people, places, times? The lottery says nothing. Chile, with near-universal coverage (FONASA, ISAPREs), would compare plans, not coverage vs. none; extrapolation is an argument you must make.

Bivariate regression I: a difference in means is a regression

Reading: S&W 4.1–4.3.

The regression model with a dummy

\[Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1D_i+u_i\]
  • \(\beta_1\): the effect of winning; \(u_i\): everything else that determines health (age, income, habits, luck).
  • We can always set \(E[u_i]=0\): any average \(c\) gets absorbed into the intercept, \(Y_i=(\beta_0+c)+\beta_1D_i+(u_i-c)\).
  • Randomization gives \(E[u_i\mid D_i=1]=E[u_i\mid D_i=0]=0\). The lottery doesn't remove age or income from \(u_i\); it balances them across groups on average.
  • Taking group means: \(E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]=\beta_0\), \(E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]=\beta_0+\beta_1\). So with random assignment \(\beta_0\) = control mean and \(\beta_1\) = difference in means. Without it, the difference in means mixes \(\beta_1\) with \(E[u_i\mid D_i=1]-E[u_i\mid D_i=0]\).
  • Oregon: \(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y_0=.548\), \(\hat\beta_1=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0=.039\).

Regression in potential-outcomes language

With a constant effect \(Y_{1i}-Y_{0i}=\beta_1\):

\[Y_i=Y_{0i}+\beta_1D_i=\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}]}_{\beta_0}+\beta_1D_i+\underbrace{Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]}_{u_i}\]

So \(E[u_i\mid D_i]=0\) is exactly \(E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=1]=E[Y_{0i}\mid D_i=0]\): no selection bias.

Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004): callback discrimination

  • Theories: taste-based (Becker 1957: employers pay to avoid a group) vs. statistical (Phelps 1972, Arrow 1973: race used as a proxy for unobserved skill).
  • You cannot randomize race, but you can randomize what employers believe: the name on a fictitious résumé. ~5,000 résumés to 1,300+ ads in Boston and Chicago (2001–02), usually four per ad, half with Black-sounding names chosen at random.
  • Why not compare Black and white workers? The observed gap = discrimination (effect on the treated) + selection bias (differences in schooling, experience, skills). Controlling for surveyed skills doesn't fix it; employers see far more than surveys record.
  • Balance: 7 of 8 résumé traits within 2 SEs; computer skills p = .03, but with 8 tests a 34% chance of at least one.
  • Callbacks: white 9.65%, Black 6.45%. Difference .0320, \(SE=\sqrt{.2953^2/2435+.2456^2/2435}=.0078\), t = 4.1, 95% CI [.017, .047]. About 10 résumés per callback for white names vs. 15 for Black names. Binary outcome: \(s=\sqrt{\bar Y(1-\bar Y)}\).
  • As a regression: \(\hat\beta_0=.0645\) (Black-name callback rate), \(\hat\beta_1=.0320\) (.0078), the average causal effect of the name.

Two design concepts: first stage and spillovers

Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer & Gentzkow (2020): a random third of 1,661 Facebook users offered $102 to deactivate for 4 weeks (580 treatment, 1,081 control).

  • First stage (take-up): how much assignment raises the probability of actually being treated. Share of checks deactivated: .90 vs. .02, first stage = .88.
  • Treatment vs. control gives the effect of the offer. If each of the 88 per 100 who deactivate because of it gains \(\Delta\) and the rest gain nothing: effect of offer = .88 × Δ, so Δ = effect of the offer ÷ first stage. Facebook app use fell 10.1 min/day from the offer, so 10.1/.88 = 11.5 min from deactivating. Assumes the offer affects outcomes only through deactivation.
  • Spillovers: one person's treatment changes another's outcome. Treat − control = effect on the treated − change in the control group. Allcott et al. randomized individuals whose friends were mostly outside the study, so their estimate is "one person quits while everyone else stays."
  • Results (SD units): well-being +0.09 (t = 2.3), news knowledge −0.19, polarization −0.16, later Facebook use −0.61. Happiness +0.12 on a 1–7 scale; loneliness not significant.
  • Observational comparison of light vs. heavy users: 0.23 SD. Experiment: 0.09. So selection bias ≈ 0.14: light users would be happier anyway.

Effect of the offer is often called the intent-to-treat effect; dividing by the first stage gives the effect on those who take up the treatment because of the offer. A low first stage blows up both the estimate and its SE, which is why designs need high take-up.

The error term vs. the residual

  • \(u_i=Y_i-(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i)\): vertical distance to the population line, never observed.
  • \(\hat u_i=Y_i-\hat Y_i\): vertical distance to our fitted line; positive above it.

Least squares

  • The sum of residuals can't rank lines (many lines give 0). Square them: in the made-up example, SSR = 18.1 (flat), 9.7 (slope 1), 21.0 (shifted), 3.0 (blue, the winner).
  • Squaring stops misses cancelling, penalizes big misses more (missing by 10 costs 4× missing by 5), and gives exact formulas.
\[\min_{\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1}\sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i\big)^2\]

Derivation (know it)

  1. \(\partial S/\partial\hat\beta_0=-2\sum(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i)=0\Rightarrow\sum\hat u_i=0\).
  2. \(\partial S/\partial\hat\beta_1=-2\sum X_i(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i)=0\Rightarrow\sum X_i\hat u_i=0\). Residuals are uncorrelated with X.
  3. Divide (1) by \(n\): \(\bar Y-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1\bar X=0\Rightarrow\hat\beta_0=\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X\). The line passes through \((\bar X,\bar Y)\).
  4. Substitute into (2): \(\sum X_i[(Y_i-\bar Y)-\hat\beta_1(X_i-\bar X)]=0\Rightarrow\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{\sum X_i(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sum X_i(X_i-\bar X)}\).
  5. Since deviations sum to zero, replace \(X_i\) by \(X_i-\bar X\): \(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}=\dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}\).

Dummy check: with \(\bar D=p\), \(\sum(D_i-p)^2=np(1-p)\) and \(\sum(D_i-p)(Y_i-\bar Y)=np(1-p)(\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0)\), so \(\hat\beta_1=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0\) and \(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y_0\). The dummy regression was least squares all along.

Decomposing the variation

\[Y_i-\bar Y=(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)+\hat u_i\quad\Rightarrow\quad\underbrace{\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{TSS}=\underbrace{\sum(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{ESS}+\underbrace{\sum\hat u_i^2}_{SSR}\]

R² and the SER

  • \(R^2=ESS/TSS=1-SSR/TSS\in[0,1]\): share of the variation in Y the line accounts for. Since TSS doesn't depend on the line, OLS (min SSR) maximizes R².
  • \(SER=\sqrt{SSR/(n-2)}\): the standard deviation of the misses around the line, an estimate of \(\sigma_u\). Stata's "Root MSE". Not the "std. err." column.

\(\hat\beta_1\) is a random variable

  • Another cohort would give a different slope. Resampling 100 of the 988 colleges 1,000 times: most slopes between $61 and $91 per SAT point, centered on 75.6. With samples of 400 the spread halves ($9.1 → $4.7); with 988, $2.9.

Property 1: unbiasedness

Substitute \(Y_i-\bar Y=\beta_1(X_i-\bar X)+(u_i-\bar u)\):

\[\hat\beta_1=\beta_1+\frac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)u_i}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\]

Holding the X's fixed, \(E[\hat\beta_1\mid X\text{'s}]=\beta_1+\dfrac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)E[u_i\mid X_i]}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}=\beta_1\) when \(E[u_i\mid X_i]=0\) (random sampling gives \(E[u_i\mid\text{all }X]=E[u_i\mid X_i]\)). Average over samples: \(E[\hat\beta_1]=\beta_1\). If \(u_i\) tends to be higher at high-SAT colleges, the sum is positive on average and \(\hat\beta_1\) is biased upward.

  • \(\bar Y\) is unbiased in any random sample; \(\hat\beta_1\) also needs \(E[u_i\mid X_i]=0\). Random assignment makes it true; nobody assigned SAT scores to colleges at random.
  • Shirt example: mom says everything looks great (biased); three honest friends averaged (unbiased).

Property 2: consistency

\[\hat\beta_1=\frac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)}{\mathrm{var}(X)}=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(X,\beta_0+\beta_1X+u)}{\mathrm{var}(X)}=\beta_1+\frac{\mathrm{cov}(X_i,u_i)}{\mathrm{var}(X_i)}\]

If \(E[u_i\mid X_i]=0\) then \(\mathrm{cov}(X_i,u_i)=0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) is consistent. If \(\mathrm{cov}(X_i,u_i)>0\), even 100,000 colleges give \(\hat\beta_1\) above \(\beta_1\): more data does not fix bias.

Inference: the same recipe as for the mean

  • CLT: \(\hat\beta_1\approx N(\beta_1,\sigma^2_{\hat\beta_1})\) with \(\sigma^2_{\hat\beta_1}=\frac1n\,\dfrac{\mathrm{var}[(X_i-\mu_X)u_i]}{(\sigma_X^2)^2}\), because the numerator \(\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)u_i\) is an average of mean-zero terms. Needs i.i.d. draws and rare large outliers.
  • \(t=(\hat\beta_1-\beta_{1,0})/SE(\hat\beta_1)\); 95% CI \(\hat\beta_1\pm1.96\,SE(\hat\beta_1)\).
  • College line: SE 2.936, t = 25.76, CI 69.86 to 81.38 per point ($6,986 to $8,138 per 100 points).

What makes \(SE(\hat\beta_1)\) small

\[SE(\hat\beta_1)\approx\frac{SER}{\sqrt n\;s_X}\quad\text{(homoskedasticity-only)}\]

Larger \(n\) (falls like \(1/\sqrt n\)), smaller SER (less noise), wider spread of X (a wide range pins the slope). College line: \(10{,}982/(\sqrt{988}\times147)\approx\$2.38\), below Stata's robust $2.94.

Heteroskedasticity and robust SEs

  • Homoskedastic: \(\mathrm{var}(u_i\mid X_i)=\sigma_u^2\), the same spread at every X. Heteroskedastic: spread varies with X. Colleges spread out more at high SAT, so heteroskedastic.
  • Robust SE estimates the CLT variance directly, weighting each \(\hat u_i^2\) by \((X_i-\bar X)^2\): misses far from the mean X count most. Here robust > homoskedastic ($2.94 vs. $2.38).
  • Always use robust: , robust in Stata; vcovHC(fit, type = "HC1") in R. Under homoskedasticity the two agree in large samples. Gauss–Markov (OLS is BLUE under homoskedasticity) is not needed.

Omitted variable bias

The question: when does a regression slope mislead, in which direction, and by how much? Reading: S&W 6.1–6.2; Mastering 'Metrics ch. 2.

Stop 1 · A number that looks like an answer

We left Lecture 9 with the college line: colleges whose admitted students score 100 points higher on the SAT have median earnings about $7,560 higher. It's tempting to read that as "going to a more selective college pays $7,560 a year." Lecture 10 asks whether we're allowed to.

The answer depends entirely on what's hiding in \(u_i\), the error term: everything else that affects earnings. If anything in there is systematically higher at high-SAT colleges, the slope is doing double duty. It measures SAT's effect plus that other thing's effect.

Slide question: You chose Columbia (SAT 1553). Would you earn $7,560 less at a college averaging 1453? List three things in u and say whether each is higher at high-SAT colleges.

Not necessarily. The $7,560 compares different colleges with different students, not you at two colleges. Things in u:

• Parents' income and connections: higher at high-SAT colleges (raises earnings).
• Mix of majors (engineering, CS, finance): higher at high-SAT colleges.
• Location in high-wage cities like New York: likely higher.
• Students' own ambition and skills beyond the SAT: likely higher.

All of these raise earnings and rise with SAT, so the $7,560 likely overstates what the college itself does for you.

In plain words

Unbiased needs \(E[u\mid X]=0\): the average of "everything else" is the same at every SAT level. If parents' income is in u and rises with SAT, then \(\mathrm{cov}(SAT,u)>0\) and the slope settles on a number above the true effect, no matter how many colleges we add.

Evidence that this is real (Chetty, Deming & Friedman 2026): among students with the same SAT/ACT score, children from the top 1% attend Ivy Plus colleges at about twice the rate of middle-class children. So parents' income is in u and moves with selectivity.

Stop 2 · Selection bias and \(E[u\mid X]=0\) are the same idea

In Lecture 5 we worried about selection bias. In Lecture 9 we worried about \(E[u\mid X]=0\). These are one idea in two languages.

Write the potential-outcomes model as a regression: with the same effect for everyone, \(Y_i=\underbrace{E[Y_{0i}]}_{\beta_0}+\beta_1D_i+\underbrace{Y_{0i}-E[Y_{0i}]}_{u_i}\). So the error term is how person i would do without treatment, compared with the average person.

Then "\(E[u\mid D=1]=E[u\mid D=0]=0\)" says exactly "\(E[Y_{0}\mid D=1]=E[Y_{0}\mid D=0]\)": the treated and untreated would have done equally well without the treatment. No selection bias. If it fails, \(\hat\beta_1=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0\) estimates \(\beta_1\) + selection bias.

Stop 3 · The two arrows

Stop 4 · How big, and which way

If the true ("long") model is \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2W+v\) but we run the "short" regression of Y on X alone:

Multiple regression

From diagnosing omitted variable bias to doing something about it. Reading: S&W 6.2–6.8.

Stop 1 · Mimic the experiment we can't run

In the ideal world we'd assign education by coin flip. Then ability would be the same on average in both groups, cov(education, ability) = 0, and the bias \(\beta_2\cdot\mathrm{cov}(Educ,Ability)/\mathrm{var}(Educ)\) would vanish.

In the real world, ability helps decide who gets educated and raises earnings. So ask the question "Why did you go to college? Why didn't your classmates?" Whatever answers it, if it also affects earnings, it's a source of bias.

The goal of regression is to mimic the experiment. If we can't randomize education, we can at least compare people with the same ability:

\[Earnings_i=\beta_0+\beta_1Education_i+\beta_2Ability_i+u_i\]

In plain words

"Holding ability constant" means: look only within groups of people who have the same ability, and compare those with more and less education there. In the picture with colored boxes (one per ability level), \(\beta_1\) comes from comparisons inside each box, not from comparing all college graduates with everyone else. If nothing else decides education within a box, education is "as good as randomly assigned" there.

Multiple regression answers two questions at once: the effect of college holding ability constant, and the effect of ability holding education constant.

Stop 2 · What's left in u?

Once ability is a regressor, it's no longer in u. But motivation, family connections, work experience, personality and field of study still are. For \(\beta_1\) to be causal, holding ability fixed, each of these must have the same average at every level of education.

Slide question: what is the assumption if we are interested in the causal effect of ability instead?

Flip the roles: holding education fixed, ability must be as good as randomly assigned. Everything left in u (motivation, family background, connections) must have the same average at every ability level among people with the same education. That's hard to believe, since family background shapes both. There's also a subtlety: ability partly works through education, so holding education fixed measures only ability's effect beyond the education it leads to.

Family background, motivation and money still decide who gets educated, so \(\hat\beta_1\) can still be biased. The fix is to add more controls: \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_k\).

Stop 3 · The model and how to read it

\[Y_i=\beta_0+\underbrace{\beta_1X_{1i}}_{\text{variable of interest}}+\underbrace{\beta_2X_{2i}+\cdots+\beta_kX_{ki}}_{\text{control variables}}+u_i\]
  • Variable of interest (X₁): the one whose causal effect we want. Controls (X₂…Xₖ): there to remove omitted variable bias from X₁. Controls don't need to be randomly assigned and their own coefficients need not be causal.
  • Intercept: \(\beta_0=E[Y\mid\text{all }X=0]\), the mean of Y when every regressor is zero (often outside the data).
  • Slope: \(\beta_1\) = change in mean Y for a one-unit increase in X₁, holding the other regressors constant at any values.
  • Estimation is still least squares: choose the β̂'s that minimize the sum of squared residuals. The formulas need matrix algebra, so Stata does it: regress Y X1 X2. Predictions and residuals work as before: \(\hat Y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1X_{1i}+\cdots\), \(\hat u_i=Y_i-\hat Y_i\).

Across these 820 colleges, those whose admitted students score one more SAT point have median earnings $77.78 higher on average ($7,778 per 100 points). Nothing is held constant, so it includes everything that moves with SAT, like parents' income.

Stop 4 · The assumption for causality, upgraded

Conditional mean independence: \(E[u_i\mid X_{1i},\dots,X_{ki}]=E[u_i]\). When we care only about X₁:

  • Holding the controls constant, X₁ is as good as randomly assigned.
  • Whatever is still omitted must be unrelated to X₁ once the controls are held fixed.
  • The controls themselves need not be randomly assigned.
Slide question: is this assumption more plausible than \(E[u_i\mid X_i]=0\) without controls?

Yes, usually more plausible: we have removed the confounders we could measure, so less is left in u. But it is still an assumption. Anything unmeasured (motivation, talent) that differs with X₁ among observations with the same controls still biases \(\hat\beta_1\). Controls make the comparison fairer; only randomization makes it fair by design.

Slide exercise: does your regression satisfy conditional mean independence? (Worked example)

(1) Question: does throwing harder lower runs allowed? Y = earned runs allowed per 9 innings, X₁ = average fastball velocity; controls: years of experience, age, quality of opponents faced.

(2) Left out: pitch movement, command, the defense behind the pitcher. Harder throwers tend to have better stuff overall, so these differ with X₁.

(3) Direction: W = command. It lowers runs (β₂ < 0) and goes with velocity (cov > 0), so the bias is negative: velocity's run-saving effect looks bigger than it is (overstated).

Stop 5 · Perfect multicollinearity

Regressors are perfectly multicollinear if one is an exact linear function of the others. Key insight: the intercept is a hidden regressor equal to 1 for everyone, \(Y_i=\beta_0\cdot1+\beta_1X_{1i}+\cdots\).

Slide question (detective work): Y is earnings. Which are perfectly multicollinear? (a) years and months of education (b) dummies for male and female (c) age and age² (d) income and wealth (e) shares of the day spent working and not working

(a) Yes: months = 12 × years.

(b) Yes: male + female = 1, the intercept's hidden regressor (the dummy variable trap).

(c) No: age² is a nonlinear function of age, so it can't be written as a linear combination.

(d) No: highly correlated but not exactly; that's imperfect multicollinearity.

(e) Yes: working + not working = 1 = the intercept.

  • Most common case: a full set of dummies that are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (every observation in exactly one category). They add up to 1, duplicating the intercept.
  • Why it's a problem: mathematically, a division by zero in the OLS formula. Intuitively, the question makes no sense: "the effect of being male, holding female constant" asks you to change one without the other.
  • Imperfect multicollinearity (income and wealth highly but not perfectly correlated): OLS works but is imprecise, with big SEs, because few observations separate the two.
  • Fix: find the culprits and drop one. With dummies, the dropped category becomes the reference category: every dummy coefficient is a difference from it.

Stop 6 · R² and adjusted R²

  • R² is the same formula as before: \(R^2=ESS/TSS=1-SSR/TSS\).
  • R² never falls when you add a regressor. OLS could always set the new coefficient to 0 and keep the old fit, and by chance it nearly always finds a little to fit. So R² flatters big models.
  • Adjusted R² penalizes extra regressors: \(\bar R^2=1-\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}\cdot\frac{SSR}{TSS}\). Since \(\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}>1\), it is always below R². Adding a regressor lowers SSR (pushes it up) but raises the penalty factor (pushes it down); it can go either way.
Slide question: can the R² be negative? Can the adjusted R² be negative?

R²: no (with an intercept). OLS can always do at least as well as predicting Ȳ for everyone, so SSR ≤ TSS and R² ≥ 0. Adjusted R²: yes. If the regressors explain almost nothing and k is large relative to n, the penalty can push it below 0.

In plain words

Don't choose controls by R². For causal questions, include what removes bias from your variable of interest, whether or not it raises R². For prediction, more regressors can fit the sample better and still predict new data worse.

What to remember

  • Multiple regression adds the omitted variable as a regressor and estimates X's effect holding the others constant.
  • Each slope is the change in mean Y for a one-unit increase in its regressor, with the others fixed.
  • Key assumption: holding the controls constant, X₁ is as good as randomly assigned.
  • Perfectly multicollinear regressors can't all go in: drop one and read the rest relative to it.
  • R² never falls when you add a regressor; adjusted R² can. Neither should decide what to include in a causal regression.

Concept checks

You add a control and the coefficient on X₁ barely changes. What does that suggest about that control?

It wasn't an important source of omitted variable bias for X₁: either it doesn't affect Y given the other regressors, or it isn't related to X₁ (like "public" in the college table).

A regression includes dummies for freshman, sophomore, junior and senior plus an intercept. What goes wrong and how do you fix it?

Perfect multicollinearity: the four dummies sum to 1, the intercept. Drop one (say freshman); the other coefficients become differences from freshmen.

Adding a regressor raised R² from 0.30 to 0.31 but lowered adjusted R². Should you drop it?

Not on that basis. If it's a confounder for your variable of interest, keep it: the goal is an unbiased coefficient, not a higher R².

Income and wealth are both in a regression; their coefficients have huge standard errors. Why?

Imperfect multicollinearity: they move together so closely that the data have little information to separate their individual effects. Estimates are imprecise, not biased.

Does controlling for more variables always reduce bias?

It removes bias from the confounders you add. It can't touch what you leave out, and adding variables that are themselves results of X₁ (like controlling for occupation when studying education's effect on earnings) can remove part of the effect you want to measure.

Tests with several regressors (S&W ch. 7)

  • One coefficient at a time: exactly as before, \(t=(\hat\beta_j-\beta_{j,0})/SE(\hat\beta_j)\), with robust SEs. Read it as "holding the other regressors fixed."
  • Several at once (\(H_0:\beta_1=\beta_2=0\)): use the F-statistic, not two separate t-tests. Two t-tests at 5% each reject a true joint null more than 5% of the time, and they ignore the correlation between the estimates. With one restriction, F = t².
  • Concept to know: a variable can be individually insignificant but jointly significant with another when the two are highly correlated (imperfect multicollinearity).

Nonlinear regression (S&W 8.1, 8.3)

  • Polynomials: \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2X^2+u\). The effect of X depends on where you start. Compute the predicted Y at two X values and subtract. A negative \(\beta_2\) means the effect shrinks as X grows (diminishing returns).
  • Logs: Lecture 8.
  • Interactions: with two dummies, \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1D_1+\beta_2D_2+\beta_3D_1D_2\), \(\beta_3\) is how much the effect of \(D_1\) changes when \(D_2=1\). With \(X\times D\), the two groups get different slopes. With \(X_1\times X_2\), the effect of \(X_1\) is \(\beta_1+\beta_3X_2\).

P2. Show E(Ȳ) = μY

If there are m odd-indexed observations, \(E\big(\frac1m\sum_{\text{odd}}Y_i\big)=\frac1m\cdot m\mu_Y=\mu_Y\): unbiased. It throws away half the data, so its variance σ²/m is about twice that of Ȳ.

True / False / Uncertain drill

Say the verdict and a one-line reason out loud, then check.

A p-value of 0.03 means there is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true.

False. It is the probability of data at least this extreme if H₀ were true.

Doubling the sample size halves the standard error of Ȳ.

False. SE ∝ 1/√n, so doubling n divides it by √2 (about 29% smaller). Quadrupling halves it.

An unbiased estimator is always consistent.

False. Y₁ alone is unbiased but its variance never shrinks.

The CLT says that with a large sample the data are approximately normal.

False. It says the sampling distribution of Ȳ is approximately normal; the data can be skewed.

In a randomized experiment, the difference in means equals the average treatment effect.

True in expectation (selection bias is zero); any one sample differs by sampling error.

If the 95% CI for β₁ includes 0, then β₁ = 0.

False. We fail to reject β₁ = 0; we don't prove it.

The OLS residuals are uncorrelated with X in every sample.

True. Σ Xiûi = 0 and Σ ûi = 0 by the first-order conditions. This says nothing about the errors ui.

Measuring X in thousands instead of units changes the t-statistic on β̂₁.

False. β̂₁ and its SE scale by the same factor; t, p and R² are unchanged.

A regression of Y on a dummy D gives β̂₀ = mean of Y in the D = 1 group.

False. β̂₀ is the mean in the D = 0 group; β̂₀ + β̂₁ is the D = 1 mean.

If var(u | X) is the same for all X, robust and homoskedasticity-only SEs give very different answers.

False. Under homoskedasticity they agree in large samples; robust SEs are safe either way.

Dividing the effect of an offer by the first stage gives the effect for everyone in the sample.

False (or Uncertain). It gives the effect for those who take up the treatment because of the offer, and requires the exclusion assumption.

A balance table with all p-values above 0.10 proves the estimate is causal.

False. It checks observed pre-treatment traits only; attrition, non-compliance and spillovers can still bias the result.

An omitted variable that affects Y but is uncorrelated with X biases β̂₁.

False. OVB needs both conditions: Z affects Y and is correlated with X. It does make the SER larger.

With ln(Y) on X, β̂₁ = 0.40 means one more unit of X raises Y by exactly 40%.

False. 100β₁% is a small-change approximation; exactly e^0.40 − 1 = 49%.

Adding a control variable that raises R² proves the control was needed to remove bias.

False. R² always rises (weakly) with a new regressor. A control removes bias only if it affects Y and is correlated with X₁; check whether the coefficient on X₁ moves.

Including dummies for all four seasons plus an intercept lets you estimate each season's effect.

False. They sum to 1, the intercept's hidden regressor: perfect multicollinearity. Drop one season; the rest are read relative to it.

In multiple regression, β₁ is the effect of X₁ holding the other regressors constant.

True as an interpretation. It is a causal effect only if, holding those regressors constant, X₁ is as good as randomly assigned.

If omitting W makes the short slope too large, then β₂ and cov(X, W) must both be positive.

False. Upward bias only needs the two signs to match: both positive or both negative.
