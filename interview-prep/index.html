04FP&A brush-ups

A recruiter won't drill you, but you should explain each of these in one or two sentences. Click to check yourself.

What does FP&A do?
Financial Planning & Analysis builds the budget, updates forecasts during the year, explains why actual results differed from plan (variance analysis), and gives leaders the numbers they need to decide where to invest. It's the forward-looking side of finance; accounting records what happened.
How do the three financial statements connect?

Income statement shows revenue minus expenses = net income over a period. Net income flows to the top of the cash flow statement, which adjusts for non-cash items (like depreciation) and changes in working capital to get the change in cash. That ending cash, plus net income added to retained earnings, lands on the balance sheet, which must balance: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

Net income → top of cash flow statement → ending cash → balance sheet Net income → retained earnings → equity on balance sheet
Budget vs. forecast vs. actuals
Budget: the plan set before the year starts. Forecast: an updated estimate during the year as conditions change. Actuals: what really happened. FP&A compares all three.
Variance analysis (with an example)

Variance = Actual − Budget. For revenue, above budget is favorable; for costs, above budget is unfavorable. The real work is explaining why: price, volume, mix, timing, or one-time items.

Budget revenue $10.0M Actual revenue $10.6M Variance +$0.6M (6% favorable) Why? 400 extra units × $1,000 = $0.4M volume price increase on existing units = $0.2M price
Organic growth (use Thermo Fisher's own numbers)

Organic growth strips out acquisitions, divestitures and currency so you see how the existing business really grew.

Q3 2026 total revenue growth 9% − impact of acquisitions/divestitures 3% − impact of currency 0% = organic growth 6%

Being able to walk through this shows you read their earnings release.

Margins: gross, operating, adjusted
Gross margin = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue Operating margin = Operating income / Revenue Adjusted (non-GAAP) = excludes items like amortization of acquired intangibles and restructuring

Thermo Fisher Q3: GAAP operating margin 18.6%, adjusted 24.5%. The gap is mostly acquisition-related amortization, typical for a company that grows through acquisitions.

EBITDA and free cash flow
EBITDA: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization; a rough measure of operating profitability. Free cash flow: operating cash flow minus capital expenditures; the cash left to pay debt, buy back stock or make acquisitions.
Working capital
Current assets minus current liabilities, mainly receivables + inventory − payables. If receivables or inventory grow, cash is tied up; that's why a fast-growing company can be profitable and still short on cash.
Accruals and month-end close
Accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash moves. Month-end close is the process of recording accruals, reconciling accounts and producing the monthly financials that FP&A then analyzes.
Excel skills to mention honestly
XLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH, pivot tables, SUMIFS, building a budget-vs-actual table with variance columns. Pair it with R and Stata from your coursework: "I'm strongest in R for analysis and comfortable building models in Excel."

06How to memorize your answers

Memorize the skeleton, not the script. Word-for-word answers sound rehearsed, and one forgotten word derails you.

  1. Reduce each story to 4 keywords. One per STAR step, e.g. "slump · rebuild mechanics · film with coach · 2.1 ERA." That's what you memorize. Write them in the notes boxes above.
  2. Lock in only two things verbatim: your first sentence and your result line. A strong open and close make the middle feel polished even if it varies.
  3. Practice out loud, never just in your head. Thinking an answer takes half the time of saying it, so silent practice fools you.
  4. Use active recall. Look at the question only, answer aloud, then check your keywords. Use the Drill below; it shuffles questions so you can't rely on order.
  5. Space it out. Three short sessions (Fri, Sat, Sun) beat one long one. Each night, run every story once before bed.
  6. Record and listen once per story. Cut filler ("um," "like," "so basically") and anything over 2 minutes.
  7. Vary the wording on purpose. Tell each story a slightly different way each time. Flexibility is what lets you bend one story to fit a question you didn't expect.
  8. Map stories to questions. Five strong stories can answer almost any behavioral question. Know which story you'd use for each one below.
On the call: it's fine to have your keyword sheet beside your laptop. Glance, don't read. Pausing two seconds before you answer looks thoughtful, not unprepared.
